PART 00. START HERE IF YOU KNOW NOTHING ABOUT FINANCE OR AI
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This part assumes you know nothing about finance and nothing about AI. Read
it first. Everything later in the guide builds on it.

1. THE STORY IN ONE PARAGRAPH

  Imagine a friend asks to borrow $1,000 and promises to pay you back next
  year with a little extra. Before saying yes you would want to know: do they
  have a job? Do they already owe other people money? Is their income going
  up or down? Who do they depend on? Professional investors do exactly this,
  but the "friend" is a company, the loan is millions or billions of dollars,
  and the answers are buried in hundreds of pages of official reports. Signal
  Watch reads those reports for them, draws the answers as pictures, writes
  the recommendation, and then argues against its own recommendation so the
  investor does not fool themselves.

2. FINANCE, FROM ZERO

  What is a company's debt?
    Companies borrow money, like people take out a mortgage or a car loan.
    They borrow from banks (loans) or from investors (bonds: a bond is an
    IOU the company sells, promising to pay interest and return the money by
    a certain date, the "maturity").

  What is a hedge fund?
    A firm that manages a large pool of money for wealthy people, pension
    funds and universities, and tries to grow it. Some hedge funds buy stocks
    (a small piece of ownership in a company). Others, called credit funds,
    mostly buy debt: loans and bonds. They earn the interest, and they lose
    money if the company cannot pay back.

  Why lending is mostly about avoiding losses
    If you lend $100 at 10%, the best case is getting $110 back. The worst
    case is losing most of the $100. The upside is small and fixed; the
    downside is large. So a lender's skill is not finding winners, it is
    spotting the companies that will not be able to pay, before everyone
    else does. That is why this whole project is about risk and warning signs.

  What does a credit analyst do all day?
    They are the people at the fund who decide whether a loan is safe. For
    each company they:
      - read its official reports (often 100-300 pages each),
      - figure out who it depends on (customers, suppliers, lenders,
        regulators),
      - check whether it makes enough cash to pay its debts,
      - list what it owes and when each debt is due,
      - compare it with similar companies,
      - write a memo (a short report) recommending what to do,
      - and watch 50 to 70 companies at once for anything that changes.
    That is hours of reading per company, every quarter.

  What are the "official reports"? (SEC filings)
    In the United States, every company whose shares trade publicly must file
    reports with a government agency, the SEC (Securities and Exchange
    Commission). They are free for anyone to read on the SEC's website,
    called EDGAR. The main ones:
      10-K   the yearly report, the longest and most complete
      10-Q   the report every three months ("quarterly")
      8-K    a short report whenever something big happens: a new loan, the
             boss quits, the auditor changes
      Form 4 filed when a company insider (an executive or director) buys or
             sells the company's own stock
    The numbers in these reports are also tagged in a computer-readable way
    (called XBRL), so a program can read "revenue was $44.8 million" without
    guessing.

  A few words you will hear constantly
    Revenue        money coming in from customers
    Cash flow      cash actually received minus cash actually spent
    Burn           spending more cash than comes in ("burning cash")
    Runway         how long the cash lasts at the current burn
    Leverage       how much debt compared with earnings
    Maturity       the date a debt must be repaid
    Quarter        a three-month period; companies report every quarter
  Part 1 has the full list.

3. AI, FROM ZERO

  What is the AI here?
    We use Gemini, a "large language model" made by Google. You give it text
    and instructions, and it gives back text. It is very good at reading a
    long document and answering "who are this company's customers?" or
    "write a summary of these facts". It is the same kind of technology as
    ChatGPT.

  What is it bad at?
    It can make things up confidently ("hallucinate"): invent a number, a
    quote or a fact that sounds right. In finance one invented number can
    cost millions, so this is the central problem we had to solve.

  So what does the AI actually do in Signal Watch, and what does it NOT do?
    The AI does the reading and the writing:
      - reads the filings and lists the companies, risks and debts named in
        them, quoting the exact sentence each came from,
      - writes the memo and the counter-argument from facts we give it.
    Our own code (ordinary programs, not AI) does everything that must be
    exact:
      - downloads the filings from the SEC,
      - checks every quote the AI gives against the real document, and throws
        away anything that is not actually there,
      - takes all numbers from the SEC's tagged data, not from the AI,
      - deletes any sentence the AI writes that contains a number we did not
        give it,
      - runs the 36 warning-sign checks and the watchlist rules.
    Think of the AI as a fast, tireless junior assistant whose every sentence
    is checked by a strict editor (our code) before anyone sees it.

4. WHY WOULD THIS BE BUILT AT A HACKATHON DESIGNED FOR HEDGE FUNDS?

  A hackathon is a short competition where teams build a working product in
  a few days. This one was set up for hedge funds, so the judges are asking:
  "Would this help an investment professional make better decisions, faster?"
  Signal Watch is our answer, and each part exists for a reason a beginner
  can follow:

  The problem the judges have
    Their analysts spend most of their time reading, not thinking. Reports
    are long, there are dozens of companies, and things change every quarter.
    Their biggest losses come from a detail someone missed in a footnote.

  How each part answers it (in plain words)
    The map (Graph tab)
      "Who does this company depend on?" drawn as a picture: the company in
      the middle, boxes around it for customers, lenders, regulators and
      risks. If one customer pays most of the bills, you see it instantly.
    The numbers (Financial tab)
      "Is the business making or losing money, and is it getting better or
      worse?" shown as charts over the last eight quarters.
    The debt (Capital tab)
      "What does it owe, to whom, and when is it due?" Debt that comes due
      soon is the moment companies most often fail to pay.
    What changed (Changes tab)
      "What is different since three months ago?" The analyst already knows
      the company; they need the difference.
    Peers (Peers tab)
      "Is this company stronger or weaker than similar companies?"
    The memo (Memo tab)
      The written recommendation, the actual thing an analyst hands to their
      bosses. Every name in it links to the evidence.
    The Devil's Advocate
      A built-in critic that argues against the memo. People tend to look for
      evidence that agrees with what they already think; this part forces the
      other side onto the table, with evidence and a plain-English
      explanation. For a lender, the reasons a loan could fail matter more
      than the reasons it could succeed. This is the heart of the project.
    The watchlist
      "Which of my 60 companies needs me today, and why?" A to-do list that
      sorts companies into Act now, Review and Quiet, each with its reason.
    Private companies
      Many borrowers do not file with the SEC at all. The analyst can upload
      the company's own documents and still get the counter-argument.
    The proof everywhere
      Every box, number and sentence links to the exact page of the filing it
      came from. Investors will not act on anything they cannot check.

  Why a judge would care, in one line each
    - It saves the expensive part of an analyst's day: reading.
    - It produces the thing they actually deliver (the memo), not just
      another chart.
    - It argues against itself, which protects against the mistakes that
      cause the biggest losses.
    - It never makes up numbers, and it says "not disclosed" when it does not
      know, which is what makes it usable for real money.
    - It uses free, official data, so a fund could try it tomorrow.

5. QUESTIONS A BEGINNER (OR A NON-FINANCE JUDGE) ASKS

  "I don't know finance. What is the point, in one sentence?"
    It helps people who lend money to companies spot the companies that may
    not pay them back, faster and with proof.

  "I don't know AI. Is the AI making the decisions?"
    No. The AI reads and drafts; our code checks everything it says; the
    human analyst decides. The tool even argues against its own draft.

  "Why not just ask ChatGPT about the company?"
    A chatbot answers from memory, which can be months old and sometimes
    wrong, and it does not show where each fact came from. Signal Watch reads
    today's official filings and links every claim to its sentence.

  "Does the AI predict the future?"
    No. The AI only reads and drafts. The forecast on the Changes tab is
    arithmetic done by our code: it repeats the company's own best, average
    and worst recent quarters of cash flow, subtracts debt as it comes due,
    and shows when the cash would run out. Every input is from the filings.

  "Who would pay for this?"
    Credit funds, banks and anyone who lends to companies or buys their
    bonds: they already pay analysts to do this reading by hand.

  "Does it replace the analyst?"
    No. It does the first few hours of reading and drafting so the analyst
    spends their time judging, questioning management and deciding.

  "What is a 'counter-argument' and why should a computer write one?"
    It is the case for the opposite conclusion. Investment teams already ask
    one person to argue against every deal, because people fall in love with
    their own ideas. A computer does it every time, for every company, using
    every piece of data, and never gets tired or politely quiet.

  "Why so many tabs?"
    They follow the steps a professional credit analyst already uses (Michael
    Gatto's seven-step process), so a professional recognizes the flow at
    once. Each tab answers one question, in the order they would ask it.

  "Why does it look so plain?"
    On purpose. Finance professionals trust tools that look like research
    reports, not advertisements. The design is sober, dense and exact.

6. HOW TO EXPLAIN THE WHOLE PROJECT TO ANYONE (30 SECONDS)

  "Lenders lose money when they miss a warning sign hidden in a company's
  reports. Signal Watch reads those reports in about a minute, draws who the
  company depends on and what it owes, writes the recommendation, and then
  argues against it, with proof for every claim, so the lender sees the
  warning signs before they lend. And it keeps watching every company they
  cover, telling them each morning which one needs attention first."

PART 0. THE IDEA IN ONE PAGE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Signal Watch is a research tool for a credit analyst at a credit hedge fund.

A credit analyst asks one question about a company: will it pay back its
debt? To answer it they read the company's SEC filings (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K), map
who the company depends on (customers, lenders, suppliers, regulators), look
at its debt and cash, and write a memo for the investment committee. It takes
hours per company and they cover 50 to 70 companies.

You type a ticker (for example WULF, TeraWulf). Signal Watch:
  1. downloads the company's latest filings, headlines and market data,
  2. asks Gemini (Google's AI model) to find the entities that matter and to
     quote the sentence each one came from,
  3. checks every quote against the actual filing and throws away anything
     that is not really there,
  4. draws the result as a map (the Graph tab), with the company in the middle,
  5. builds the debt picture, a five-section memo, a comparison with last
     quarter, a peer comparison, and a "Devil's Advocate" that argues against
     the memo.

The one rule behind everything: every claim on screen must link back to the
exact sentence or tagged number in a filing. If the data is not there, the app
says "Not disclosed" instead of guessing.

PART 0.5  WHY EVERY PIECE EXISTS, AND HOW IT ALL COMES TOGETHER
          (the most important part of this guide)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Start with the user. A credit analyst decides whether to lend to a company (or
buy its bonds). Everything on screen answers one of the questions they must
answer, in the order a credit analyst works. The tabs follow Michael Gatto's
seven-step credit process, which is why each tab has a "Step X" label.

  The analyst's question                         Where Signal Watch answers it
  ---------------------------------------------  -----------------------------
  Which companies need my attention today?       Dashboard (watchlist alerts)
  Who does this company depend on?               Graph tab (Step 2)
  Does the business make money and cash?         Financial tab (Step 3a)
  What does it owe, to whom, and when?           Capital tab (Step 3c)
  What changed since last quarter?               Changes tab + the timeline (Step 4)
  Is it weaker or stronger than its peers?       Peers tab (Step 5)
  So what do we do?                              Memo tab (Step 7)
  What could make that conclusion wrong?         Devil's Advocate (everywhere)
  What do I ask management?                      Meeting brief
  Quick question about this company?             Ask (Cmd+K)
  Can I trust any of this?                       Quotes, source links, status bar

THE GRAPH (Step 2, qualitative analysis)
  Why: credit risk lives in relationships. A company with one customer worth a
  third of revenue, a guarantee that only covers one tenant, a lender that can
  pull a credit line, or a regulator that can freeze permits is riskier than
  its balance sheet shows. "Qualitative analysis" is exactly this: the
  business, customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, and risks, before
  looking at numbers.
  How it shows it:
    - The company sits in the middle; every box is something it depends on.
    - The box's border color is the type (blue company, red risk, amber
      macro, green financial, violet regulatory).
    - The left strip and the dots are severity: how much it matters to credit.
    - The line label is the relationship (CUSTOMER OF, LENDS TO, GUARANTEES)
      with its figure and, for debt, the year it is due (red within two
      years). Lines with a dollar figure are thicker the bigger the amount;
      others are thicker the more severe the box.
    - Hovering a box opens a card with its numbers as charts (Part 0.6).
    - The bar inside a box compares its figure with the others (for example
      share of revenue); the thin line at the bottom is materiality.
    - Clicking a box opens the exact filing sentence it came from.
  Why a map instead of a list: an analyst can see concentration at a glance
  (three boxes all tied to one customer) and follow chains (a guarantor that
  backs a tenant that pays the company).

TIME (the timeline bar, the quarter selector, the bars under each box)
  Why: a credit view is about direction, not a snapshot. The same risk means
  something different if it appeared this quarter versus three years ago.
    - The time bar across the top lists the last twelve 10-Q/10-K filings.
    - The small segments under each box show which of those filings name it.
      A risk named only in the latest filing is new; one named every quarter
      is persistent.
    - "As of Q2 2026, 10-Q filed 2026-08-05" appears on every tab so nobody
      mistakes old numbers for current ones.
    - The quarter range also sets how much history the Financial tab charts.
    - The market strip shows the 10-year Treasury, the share price and the
      S&P 500 over the same window, so moves can be read in context.

FINANCIAL (Step 3a, profitability)
  Why: a company repays debt from cash it generates. Revenue, EBITDA, margin,
  cash and the current ratio, with quarterly trends from XBRL, answer "can the
  business carry its debt?" Ratios such as debt/EBITDA and coverage are the
  numbers a credit committee asks for first. Five charts (revenue and EBITDA,
  margin, free cash flow, cash, current ratio) show eight quarters with value
  axes and a readout of each quarter's change from the prior quarter and from
  a year earlier.

CAPITAL (Step 3c, capital structure)
  Why: "what does it owe" decides who gets paid if things go wrong. Every
  instrument with amount, maturity, coupon and seniority; the maturity wall
  shows refinancing risk (a big year of debt coming due is where companies
  default); secured share shows how much ranks ahead of unsecured lenders.

CHANGES (Step 4)
  Why: analysts already know the company from last quarter; what they need
  is the delta. New counterparties, dropped ones, new debt, repaid debt.

PEERS (Step 5, relative value)
  Why: a 5x leverage ratio is high or low only next to comparable companies.
  Shared customers and shared risks across peers also reveal sector-wide
  exposure.

MEMO (Step 7)
  Why: the memo is the actual work product the analyst hands to the
  investment committee. Signal Watch is a writer: everything else on screen is
  the evidence for the memo, and every name in it links back to its box.

DEVIL'S ADVOCATE
  Why: every AI tool agrees with you; this one argues against its own memo.
  Confirmation bias is how funds lose money, and credit losses usually come
  from a missed detail in a footnote. 36 checks (receivables, insider selling,
  guarantee scope, maturities...) run on the real data. Then Gemini writes a
  long, structured case against the memo that uses everything the app
  gathered: the quarter-by-quarter figures, the debt, the map, what changed,
  insider trades, 8-Ks, peers and the stock price. Each point is explained in
  plain English for someone who does not know credit. It appears in the top
  bar, beside the memo, below the memo as the full case, as a "Counter view"
  on the map, and at the top of each tab.

BRIEF, DASHBOARD, ASK
  Brief: analysts meet management; the brief turns the gaps in the report
  into questions. Dashboard: analysts cover 50-70 names; the watchlist flags
  companies that filed something new. Ask: quick answers drawn only from the
  report, with citations.

TRUST LAYER (quotes, verification, status bar)
  Why: a hedge fund will not act on an AI summary it cannot check. Every box
  carries its quote, page and filing link; "17/20 verified, 3 rejected" in
  the status bar shows how many AI proposals failed the quote check.

HOW IT ALL COMES TOGETHER
  One pipeline (lib/analyze.ts) reads the filings once and produces one
  Report object. Every tab is a different view of that same Report, so the
  graph, the numbers, the memo and the counter-argument always agree and all
  point to the same sources. A typical session reads left to right:

    Dashboard alert -> open the company -> Graph (who matters)
    -> Financial (can it pay) -> Capital (what it owes, when)
    -> Changes + time (what moved) -> Peers (versus others)
    -> Memo (decision) beside the Devil's Advocate (what could break it)
    -> Brief (questions for management).

  The data layer (EDGAR, XBRL, news, market) feeds the AI layer (Gemini reads
  and classifies), which feeds the verification layer (quotes and figures
  checked), which feeds storage (Tiger Postgres) and finally the screens.

PART 0.6  WHY EACH FEATURE IS INCLUDED (feature by feature)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The test for every feature in this app: does it help a credit analyst decide
whether the company can repay its debt, faster, without having to trust the
software blindly? If a feature failed that test it was left out. This part
goes through what is on screen and gives the reason for each piece. Judges
ask "why is this here?" about almost everything; this is the answer sheet.

THE GOAL, IN ONE SENTENCE
  Do the hours of filing-reading a credit analyst does for each company, show
  the result in the order an analyst thinks, prove every claim, and then
  argue against our own conclusion so the analyst does not fall for it.

  Four principles follow from that sentence, and every feature serves one:
    1. Speed:      hours of reading become about a minute.
    2. Structure:  the screens follow the analyst's own process.
    3. Proof:      every claim links to the sentence or number it came from.
    4. Challenge:  the app argues against its own memo.

TOP BAR AND NAVIGATION
  Ticker box            Speed. One input; the analyst already knows the
                        ticker. It clears after Analyze so the next ticker can
                        be typed straight away.
  Tagline and logo      The logo returns home; the tagline says the pitch in
                        six words ("The credit analyst's workflow, automated").
  Memo/concerns badge   "Memo: Stable · 7 concerns". Challenge. The memo's view
                        and the number of sourced concerns against it are
                        always visible together, on every tab, so nobody reads
                        "Stable" without also reading "7 concerns".
  Changes badge         Speed. A red count of facts that changed since last
                        quarter: the first thing an analyst who already knows
                        the company wants.
  Cmd+K (Ask)           Speed. A quick question ("who guarantees the leases?")
                        answered only from this report, with citations.
  Watchlist             The morning queue (see "THE WATCHLIST" below): which
                        watched companies need attention today, and why. The
                        top bar shows how many are in Act now or Review.
  Watch button          On every report: one click adds the company to the
                        queue.
  Light/dark theme      Analysts work long hours; printing and projecting at a
                        demo need the light theme.
  URL holds the state   ?report=...&tab=... means any view can be shared as a
                        link, and Back/Forward work between companies.
  Step labels           "Step 2 · Qualitative Analysis". Structure. Each tab is
                        one step of Gatto's seven-step credit process, so a
                        credit professional recognizes the flow immediately.
  Status bar            Proof. "17/20 verified · 3 rejected" shows how many AI
                        proposals failed the quote check. Showing our own
                        rejections is what makes the rest believable.

THE GRAPH TAB (each visual element has a job)
  Company in the middle       Every box is something the company depends on;
                              the picture is literally "who can hurt this
                              borrower".
  Fixed layout (not physics)  Boxes are placed on four sides by type and never
                              drift, so the map reads the same every time and
                              in screenshots.
  Border color = type         Blue company, red risk, amber macro, green
                              financial, violet regulatory, gray other. Six
                              colors only, so the color always means one thing.
  Left strip + dots = severity  How much the item matters to credit, as a
                              category (high/medium/low), never a fake score.
  Figure and bar in the box   The number the filing gives (378 MW, $3.20B,
                              23% of revenue) and how it compares to the other
                              boxes: size matters more than names.
  Thin line at the bottom     Materiality: share of revenue or of total debt,
                              only when the filing gives the denominator.
  Box width                   Material items are drawn wider, so the eye lands
                              on what is big.
  Time strip under each box   Which of the last twelve filings name it: new
                              risk versus persistent risk.
  Green/amber dot (corner)    New or changed since last quarter.
  Confidence dots             How sure we are the relationship is real, from
                              the source and wording.
  Line label                  The typed relationship (CUSTOMER OF, LENDS TO,
                              GUARANTEES), its figure, and for debt the year
                              it is due. Red when debt is due within two years,
                              because that is refinancing risk.
  Line thickness = dollars    A $3.2B bond line is visibly heavier than a $13M
                              one. Without money, thickness follows severity.
  Hover card                  Rest on a box and its numbers appear as charts:
                              for the company, eight quarters of revenue,
                              margin and cash plus the credit ratios; for any
                              other box, its figure and share, debt terms,
                              which filings name it, its severity change and
                              its quote. The map stops being a diagram and
                              becomes a way into the numbers.
  Revenue bars in the company box  The company's eight-quarter revenue trend
                              is visible before you hover anything.
  Click a box -> side panel   Proof. The exact filing sentence, page and link.
  Legend filters              Fade types or severities to see one layer
                              (only the risks, only the financing).
  Counter view                Challenge. Turns the boxes the Devil's Advocate
                              cites red and fades the rest: "where is the case
                              against this company on the map?"
  Load animation              The company appears, one ring spreads, boxes fade
                              in by severity, lines draw outward. It plays once
                              and stops: it shows the order of importance and
                              never distracts afterwards.
  Market strip                10-year Treasury, the share price and the S&P 500
                              over the same window, so moves read in context.
                              It says "no bond price feed" rather than invent
                              a spread.

THE FINANCIAL TAB
  Summary tiles     Last-twelve-month revenue, EBITDA, margin, debt, net debt
                    and cash: the six numbers a credit committee asks first.
  Quarterly charts  Revenue and EBITDA, EBITDA margin, free cash flow, cash,
                    and the current ratio against a 1.0x line. Credit is about
                    direction, so each chart shows eight quarters, the latest
                    figure, its change from a year earlier, the high and low,
                    and a readout of any quarter you hover (value, change from
                    the prior quarter and from a year earlier).
  Why free cash flow  It is the cash left to repay lenders after running the
                    business and building it. A company can show revenue
                    growth while burning cash; this chart makes that visible.
  Why the 1.0x line  Below it, bills due within a year exceed assets that turn
                    into cash within a year. One glance tells you if short-
                    term liquidity is a problem.
  Credit metric bars  Leverage, coverage and current ratio against common
                    rule-of-thumb bands, with peers as hollow markers.
  "n/m"             When EBITDA is negative, leverage is not meaningful; we say
                    so rather than print a nonsense ratio.

THE CAPITAL TAB
  Every instrument with amount, maturity, rate and ranking (who is repaid
  first), the maturity wall (how much comes due each year), and secured share.
  This is "what does it owe, to whom, and when", the core of credit.

THE CHANGES TAB
  New and dropped counterparties, new and repaid debt, changed figures since
  the previous quarter's filings. Analysts already know the company; the
  delta is what they need.

THE PEERS TAB
  Leverage, coverage and liquidity side by side, plus customers and risks the
  companies share. A ratio means little until it is compared.

THE MEMO TAB
  The five-section memo is the deliverable an analyst actually hands to the
  investment committee. The Devil's Advocate sits beside it and below it
  (Part 10.5 explains why). "Copy memo" exports both as text.

THE WATCHLIST (/dashboard): HOW IT HELPS A HEDGE-FUND ANALYST
  The job it does
    A credit analyst covers 50-70 companies. Each morning the real question is
    not "tell me about WULF" but "which of my 60 companies changed overnight,
    and which one do I deal with first?". Missing an 8-K about a debt
    acceleration, or not noticing a borrower running out of cash, is how
    losses start. The watchlist answers that question in one screen.
  How it works
    - Each watched company is checked against EDGAR (new 10-Qs, 10-Ks and
      8-Ks since its report, with 8-K item codes in words) and its XBRL
      figures (cash and free cash flow by quarter).
    - Written rules place it in Act now, Review or Quiet, and every rule that
      applies is shown as a reason with its date and a link to the source:
        Act now: an 8-K for bankruptcy, debt acceleration, a delisting notice
          or unreliable earlier statements; cash covering fewer than four
          quarters of the latest burn; debt due within a year exceeding cash.
        Review: a new 10-Q or 10-K; an 8-K about new debt, an auditor change,
          an officer leaving, a material agreement or an impairment; four to
          eight quarters of runway; debt due within two years; the memo saying
          Stable while the Devil's Advocate makes three or more strong points;
          the stock down 20% and well behind the S&P 500; cash down 25% in a
          quarter; a watch item now checkable.
      There is no score; the reasons are the explanation. On real data WULF and
      CIFR land in Act now (about 2.6 and 1.3 quarters of cash at the current
      burn), Apple in Review (Stable memo, four strong counter-points), Ford in
      Quiet.
    - Runway (quarters of cash at the latest burn rate) is computed by code:
      cash at quarter end divided by the latest quarter's negative free cash
      flow. It is the plainest measure of "how long until they need money".
    - The "What to watch, and when" items from each company's Devil's
      Advocate case are tracked: Pending until a filing that can settle them
      arrives (or their date passes), then "Ready to check", which moves the
      company into Review. The case written last quarter becomes this
      quarter's checklist.
    - The activity feed lists, across every watched company, new filings,
      important 8-Ks, insider trades (grouped per person per day) and debt
      due dates coming up, with a "new since your last visit" marker.
    - "Re-read filings and argue again" rebuilds the report from the new
      filings and rewrites the Devil's Advocate case.
  Why it is built this way
    - A queue, not a grid of cards: the analyst's question is "what first?".
    - Rules, not a score: an analyst must be able to say why a name is at the
      top, and check it against the filing.
    - Tied to the Devil's Advocate: the watchlist is where the case against
      each memo keeps working after the memo is written.
    - Built into the app: a Watch button on every report, a count in the top
      bar, and the queue on the home page, so it is part of the daily flow,
      not a page you have to remember to visit.
  Code: lib/dashboard.ts (rules, feed), app/api/watchlist/route.ts (cached for
  five minutes), app/dashboard/page.tsx (the page).

MEETING BRIEF
  One printable page: three things to know, the key numbers, questions to ask
  management built from the gaps in the report, and risks to watch.

PRIVATE COMPANY PAGE (/private)
  Many borrowers in credit funds are private and file nothing with the SEC.
  The Devil's Advocate runs on documents the analyst uploads instead
  (Part 10.5 has the details).

DESIGN CHOICES, AND WHY
  Sober, dense, no decoration      Finance users distrust tools that look
                                   like marketing. Shadows, glow, emoji and
                                   gradient text were banned; the look borrows
                                   from terminals and research reports.
  Boxes, not circles               Matches the brand (the shield logo, the
                                   square markers) and keeps the map orderly.
  Monospace, tabular figures       Numbers line up in columns so they can be
                                   compared down a table.
  Page texture (grain + faint grid)  Depth without decoration: it reads as
                                   graph paper, the analyst's own medium.
  Red as the only accent           Red means risk and the Devil's Advocate;
                                   it is never used for decoration.
  Animations play once             They show order (what loads first matters
                                   most) and then get out of the way.
  "Not disclosed" everywhere       An honest gap is more useful to a lender
                                   than a confident guess.

PART 0.7  EVERY ARROW, SYMBOL AND COLOR: WHAT IT MEANS AND WHY IT IS THERE
          ("what do the arrows mean?", "why did you put this?")
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Judges click around and point at things. This part is the lookup table. The
same answers are in the app's legend (the "?" button in the bottom-right
corner of the map).

A. ARROWS ON THE MAP LINES
  What they mean
    The arrowhead tells you which way to read the label on the line. Read
    from the end without the arrow, through the label, to the end with the
    arrow:
      Anthropic ---CUSTOMER OF--->  TeraWulf    "Anthropic is a customer of
                                                 TeraWulf"
      TeraWulf  ---REGULATED BY--->  Kentucky PSC  "TeraWulf is regulated by
                                                 the Kentucky PSC"
    So the arrow points at the company when the box acts on the company
    (customer, supplier, lender, guarantor, owner), and points at the box
    when the company is the one acted on (regulated by, exposed to,
    dependent on, partners with, competes with).
  Why we did it
    A label such as "CUSTOMER OF" is ambiguous without a direction: is the box
    our customer, or are we its customer? For credit that is the difference
    between money coming in and money going out. The arrow removes the doubt.
  Where in code
    arrowTowardCompany() in lib/node-visuals.ts decides the end; the
    arrowheads are drawn in components/map/signal-edge.tsx.

B. EVERYTHING ELSE ON A LINE

                        $3.20B), then "due 2030" for debt. Why: the line says
                        what the link is and how big it is without a click.
  Red label border and  Debt due within two years. Why: that is when a company
  red "due" year        must refinance, which is when defaults happen.
  Line thickness        With a dollar figure, 1px to 6px by the amount (square
                        root, so a $3.2B bond is clearly heavier but does not

                        should go to the biggest exposures first.
  One dot travelling    On high-severity lines, one dot runs from the company
  along a line (once)   to the box when the map loads, then stops. Why: it

PART 0.8  THE PRESENTATION TOUR (what to say at each stop, and why it is there)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The app has a presentation mode. Click "Tour" in the top bar, or "Take the
tour" on the home page. It opens the WULF example, switches tabs for you,
outlines the part being discussed in red, and shows a card with what you are
looking at, the credit fund's problem it solves, why it is there, and a line
to say. Use the arrow keys (or Back and Next) to move; Esc ends it. The page
stays usable during the tour, so you can hover the map or click a box while
you talk.

It always runs on the WULF example, so the lines (for example "7 concerns",
"17/20 verified", "minus $992 million") match what is on screen.

How to use it in a demo
  - Read the "Say it like this" line, then point at the outlined part.
  - The "Why it is here" text is the answer if a judge asks "why did you
    include this?". It is about the reason, not the technology.
  - Stop 4: hover the company box while you talk; the charts appear.
  - Stop 12: scroll the full case a little to show the evidence tables.
  - Stops 2-4 are the big picture: the fund's four problems, how everything
    flows, and the tech stack. Stops 5 onward walk through the screens.
  - The whole tour takes three to four minutes at a calm pace.

The script (the same text the tour shows; it lives in lib/tour-steps.ts)

STOP 1. THE PROBLEM WE ARE SOLVING
  What you are looking at
    TeraWulf (WULF), a company building data centers for AI, which has
    borrowed about $5.7 billion.
  Why it is here
    A lender's best case is getting paid back with interest; the worst case is
    losing most of the loan. Lenders lose money when they miss a warning sign
    buried in hundreds of pages of company reports. An analyst covers 50 to 70
    companies and cannot read everything.
  Say it like this
    "Lenders don't lose money on the headline. They lose it on the footnote
    nobody had time to read. Signal Watch reads every footnote, for every
    company, in about a minute."

STOP 2. THE FOUR PROBLEMS A CREDIT FUND HAS
  What you are looking at
    Before the product: the problems it was built to solve. Every stop after
    this answers at least one of them.
  Why it is here
    1. Too much to read: 50 to 70 companies, 100 to 300 pages each, every
    quarter, and new deals arriving faster than anyone can check the details.
    2. Losses hide in details: a guarantee that only starts once a building is
    finished, financing that is not counted as debt, sales booked but not
    collected. 3. Conviction bias: once a memo says yes, the evidence against
    it gets explained away. 4. Things change between reviews: an 8-K, insiders
    selling, cash running out, and nobody notices until the next quarter.
  Say it like this
    "Credit funds don't have a data problem. They have a reading problem, a
    details problem, a bias problem and a monitoring problem. We built one
    feature for each."

STOP 3. HOW EVERYTHING FLOWS
  What you are looking at
    Type a ticker. The app downloads the company's official reports, its
    tagged numbers, recent news and prices. The AI reads them and proposes
    what matters. Our code checks every quote and number against the source.
    The result is saved as one report, and every tab is a view of that same
    report. The Devil's Advocate then argues against it, and the watchlist
    keeps checking for anything new.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Trust and speed together: one pass of reading, one checked set of facts,
    and every screen agreeing with every other screen.
  Why it is here
    Because everything comes from one checked report, the map, the charts, the
    memo and the case against it can never contradict each other, and every
    claim on any screen leads back to the same sentence in the same filing.
  Say it like this
    "One read of the filings, one set of checked facts, many views of it, and
    a critic and a watchlist on top. Nothing on screen comes from anywhere
    else."

STOP 4. THE TECH STACK, AND WHY EACH PIECE
  What you are looking at
    Next.js and TypeScript for the app; Google Gemini to read and draft; our
    own checking code; SEC EDGAR and XBRL for the data; Postgres (Tiger) to
    save reports and the relationship graph; Vercel to host; React Flow for
    the map and our own charts.
  The fund's problem it solves
    A hackathon timeline, numbers that must be traceable, no paid data, and AI
    calls that take up to a minute.
  Why it is here
    One Next.js app holds both the screens and the server, so there is one
    thing to build and deploy. Gemini reads long filings and answers in a
    fixed format our code can check; it never supplies a number. EDGAR and
    XBRL are free, official and legally required to be accurate, so anyone can
    verify them. Postgres stores each report and every relationship, so repeat
    views are instant and relationships can be searched across companies.
    Vercel allows the long-running AI calls.
  Say it like this
    "Every choice serves trust or speed: official free data, an AI that only
    reads and drafts, code that checks it, and one app we could build and ship
    in days."

STOP 5. THE STEPS AN ANALYST ALREADY FOLLOWS
  What you are looking at
    Six tabs, each labeled with a step: qualitative analysis, profitability,
    capital structure, forecasting, valuation, recommendation.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Too much to read. A tool that makes analysts learn a new way of working
    gets ignored; one that follows their own process saves time from the first
    minute.
  Why it is here
    Credit analysts are trained on a standard seven-step process. We laid the
    app out in that order so a professional recognizes it at once and nothing
    feels new to learn. Each tab answers one question, in the order they would
    ask it.
  Say it like this
    "We didn't invent a new workflow. We automated the one credit analysts are
    already trained on, one step per tab."

STOP 6. WHO THE COMPANY DEPENDS ON   (Graph tab)
  What you are looking at
    The company in the middle. Around it: its customers, lenders, regulators
    and risks, each pulled from its official reports.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Losses hide in details. Whether a guarantee covers construction, whether
    one customer pays most of the bills, whether the tenant is a giant or a
    startup: these live in the relationships, not in the totals.
  Why it is here
    A company's risk lives in its relationships: one customer paying most of
    the bills, a guarantee with conditions, a regulator that can freeze
    permits. A list hides that; a picture shows it at a glance. Color tells
    you the type, the left strip how serious it is.
  Say it like this
    "Before any number, you see who can hurt this borrower. Every box is a
    sentence from the company's own filing, and you can click through to it."

STOP 7. THE NUMBERS, ONE HOVER AWAY   (Graph tab)
  What you are looking at
    Hover the company box: eight quarters of revenue, profit margin and cash
    appear beside it. Hover any other box for its figure, its terms and when
    it first appeared.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Too much to read. The analyst should not have to switch screens to connect
    a relationship to its dollars.
  Why it is here
    The map shows relationships; the hover brings the money to them, so the
    picture is never just a diagram. Thicker lines carry more money, and debt
    due within two years is marked in red because that is when companies most
    often fail to pay.
  Say it like this
    "The map isn't decoration. Hover anything and it tells you how much money
    is involved and how that has changed over time."

STOP 8. TIME: IS IT GETTING BETTER OR WORSE?   (Graph tab)
  What you are looking at
    The last twelve quarterly reports along the top, and the market over the
    same period.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Things change between reviews. A snapshot hides direction; the fund needs
    to know what is new and what is getting worse.
  Why it is here
    Lending is about direction. The same risk means something very different
    if it appeared this quarter or has been there for three years. Every
    screen says which report and which quarter its numbers come from, so
    nobody mistakes old news for new.
  Say it like this
    "Every number on screen is dated. You always know which quarter you are
    looking at, and whether a risk is new."

STOP 9. CAN THE BUSINESS PAY ITS DEBTS?   (Financial tab)
  What you are looking at
    Revenue, profit, cash flow, cash and short-term bills, quarter by quarter.
    Red bars are quarters that lost money or burned cash.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Losses hide in details. Growing revenue can hide a company running out of
    cash; for a lender, cash is what repays the loan.
  Why it is here
    A company repays debt from the cash it generates. These charts answer the
    first question any lender asks. Free cash flow is here because a company
    can grow sales while running out of money; for WULF the red bars make that
    impossible to miss.
  Say it like this
    "Revenue went up last quarter. Free cash flow was minus $992 million. That
    gap is the story a lender needs to see."

STOP 10. WHAT IT OWES, AND WHEN   (Capital tab)
  What you are looking at
    Every loan and bond: how much, the interest rate, who gets paid first, and
    the year it is due.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Losses hide in details. A large debt due in one year, or debt that ranks
    behind other lenders, decides who is repaid when things go wrong.
  Why it is here
    Companies rarely fail on an ordinary day; they fail when a large debt
    comes due and nobody will lend again. Knowing the amounts and dates, and
    who is repaid first if things go wrong, is the core of credit.
  Say it like this
    "This is the calendar of when the company has to find money, and who
    stands first in line if it can't."

STOP 11. WHAT CHANGED, AND WHERE IT IS HEADING   (Changes tab)
  What you are looking at
    A forecast of the company's cash for the next two years under three cases,
    then new customers, lost customers, new debt and changed figures since the
    previous report.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Things change between reviews, and lending decisions are made on the
    downside case, which is where a credit fund makes or loses money.
  Why it is here
    Gatto's Step 4 is forecasting with a management, base and downside case.
    The AI does not predict anything: our code repeats the company's own best,
    average and worst recent quarters of cash flow, subtracts debt as it comes
    due, and shows when the cash runs out. Below it, the analyst sees only
    what changed since last quarter.
  Say it like this
    "We don't let AI guess the future. If TeraWulf just keeps doing what it
    did on average last year, the cash runs out by mid-2027, and every input
    is from its own filings."

STOP 12. STRONGER OR WEAKER THAN ITS PEERS?   (Peers tab)
  What you are looking at
    Similar companies side by side: debt, earnings, cash and the customers and
    risks they share.
  The fund's problem it solves
    When a whole industry is under pressure, the fund's skill is picking which
    companies come out the other side. That needs a side-by-side comparison.
  Why it is here
    A number means little alone. Debt of five times earnings is high in one
    industry and normal in another. Comparing with similar companies turns a
    number into a judgment, and shared customers show risk across a whole
    sector.
  Say it like this
    "Is this borrower the weakest in its group or the strongest? You can't
    tell from one company alone."

STOP 13. THE RECOMMENDATION   (Memo tab)
  What you are looking at
    A short written memo: summary, thesis, key risks, what offsets them, and
    how the market sees it.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Too much to read. The analyst's real output is the memo; drafting it from
    evidence saves the most expensive hours of the week.
  Why it is here
    The memo is what an analyst actually hands to the people who decide. Most
    tools stop at showing data; we produce the deliverable itself, and every
    name in it links back to its evidence.
  Say it like this
    "This is the actual work product. Not another dashboard: the memo the
    analyst would write, with every claim linked to its source."

STOP 14. THE DEVIL'S ADVOCATE: THE CASE AGAINST   (Memo tab)
  What you are looking at
    Beside the memo, a critic that argues against it: the bottom line, each
    point and how strong it is, and what to watch.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Conviction bias. The memo argues for itself, and the details that break a
    deal get explained away. The fund needs the other side argued every time.
  Why it is here
    People look for evidence that agrees with what they already believe, and
    an AI that writes a confident memo makes that worse. Investment committees
    already ask one person to argue against every deal. We built that person
    in, so the case against is always on the table.
  Say it like this
    "Every AI tool agrees with you. Ours argues against its own memo, because
    the reasons a loan fails matter more than the reasons it succeeds."

STOP 15. THE FULL CASE, WITH PROOF AND PLAIN ENGLISH   (Memo tab)
  What you are looking at
    Each point with what the memo said, the argument with dates and quarters,
    a plain-English version, why it matters to a lender, and the evidence
    tables.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Losses hide in details, and conviction bias hides them further. The case
    digs the details out and makes the committee face them.
  Why it is here
    A warning nobody understands gets ignored, so every point is explained for
    someone with no finance background. A warning nobody can check gets
    dismissed, so every point shows its evidence. And it includes the best
    answer to itself, because a critic that only attacks is easy to wave away.
  Say it like this
    "It found that Google's guarantee only starts once the buildings are
    finished, so the riskiest period, construction, isn't covered. The memo,
    written from the same filings, said Stable."

STOP 16. BOTH VIEWS, ALWAYS VISIBLE
  What you are looking at
    "Memo: Stable · 7 concerns" in the top bar, on every tab.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Conviction bias. A single word like Stable is persuasive; seeing the
    number of objections beside it keeps judgment honest.
  Why it is here
    Nobody should read the word Stable without also seeing how many strong
    objections exist. The two views travel together wherever the analyst goes.
  Say it like this
    "You can never see the memo's verdict without seeing the case against it
    right next to it."

STOP 17. WHERE THE RISK SITS ON THE MAP   (Graph tab)
  What you are looking at
    Counter view turns the boxes the critic cites red and fades the rest.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Losses hide in details. The objections point at specific relationships: a
    guarantor, a tenant, a lender.
  Why it is here
    The objections are not abstract: they point at specific customers, lenders
    and guarantees. Showing them on the map connects the argument to the
    relationships behind it.
  Say it like this
    "One click and the map shows you exactly which relationships the case
    against is about."

STOP 18. PROOF, INCLUDING OUR OWN MISTAKES
  What you are looking at
    "17/20 verified · 3 rejected": how many of the AI's findings passed our
    quote check, and how many we threw away.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Trust. A fund will not move money on a summary it cannot check, and AI can
    invent facts.
  Why it is here
    Finance professionals will not act on anything they cannot check. Every
    box links to its exact sentence, numbers come from the government's tagged
    data, and when something is not disclosed the app says so instead of
    guessing. Showing what we rejected is what makes the rest believable.
  Say it like this
    "We show our failures on purpose. Three findings didn't match the filing
    word for word, so we threw them out, and we tell you that."

STOP 19. EVERY MORNING: WHO NEEDS ME TODAY?
  What you are looking at
    The watchlist: every company you follow, sorted into Act now, Review and
    Quiet, with the reason for each.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Things change between reviews, across 50 to 70 companies at once. Missing
    one 8-K or one borrower running out of cash is how losses start.
  Why it is here
    An analyst follows dozens of companies. The real morning question is which
    one changed overnight and which to deal with first. Each company is placed
    by plain rules (running low on cash, a dangerous filing, debt due soon,
    the critic disagreeing with the memo) and the reason is always shown.
  Say it like this
    "This is the to-do list a credit analyst actually needs: which companies
    need me today, and exactly why."

STOP 20. PRIVATE COMPANIES, AND THE TAKEAWAY
  What you are looking at
    Many borrowers file nothing publicly. The analyst can upload the company's
    own documents and still get the case against the deal, with nothing
    stored.
  The fund's problem it solves
    Some of the worst credit losses come from private borrowers with no public
    filings, where hidden financing and mismanagement go unnoticed.
  Why it is here
    Some of the most painful credit losses happen at private companies, where
    there are no public filings to read. The critic works on whatever
    documents the lender has.
  Say it like this
    "Signal Watch reads every report, shows who the borrower depends on and
    what it owes, writes the memo, argues against it with proof, and keeps
    watching. So the lender sees the warning signs before they lend, not
    after."

PART 0.10  HOW SIGNAL WATCH FOLLOWS GATTO'S SEVEN-STEP CREDIT PROCESS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Michael Gatto's seven steps are the standard way a credit analyst works a
deal. Signal Watch is laid out in that order, and the tabs carry the step
labels. Below is each step, what the app does for it, and an honest
coverage mark:
  [FULL]     the app does this step's work
  [PARTIAL]  the app does part of it; the rest is noted
  [NOT YET]  not built; say so if asked

STEP 1. SOURCES AND USES                                         [PARTIAL]
  The question: why is the company borrowing, where does the money go, and
  what type of debt is it?
  Where it is in the app
    - Capital tab: every instrument with its type (revolver, term loan,
      secured notes, unsecured notes, convertible notes), amount, rate and
      maturity, and a "Recent change" line for issuances, amendments and
      refinancings in the last twelve months, quoted from the debt note.
    - 8-K item 2.03 ("took on a new debt obligation") in the watchlist feed
      and the Devil's Advocate timeline; new and repaid debt on Changes.
    - The map shows what the money is building: for WULF, the data center
      campuses, tenants and power contracts.
  Not yet: a formal sources-and-uses table (use of proceeds line by line).

STEP 2. QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS                       [FULL]  Graph tab, "Step 2"
  The question: what are the industry and business risks?
  Where it is in the app: the entity map. Gatto's eight categories and where
  each shows up:
    Customer concentration     Customer boxes with revenue share; checks
                               "Concentration with undisclosed terms",
                               "Anchor tenant loss risk", "Rev % n/d".
    Product concentration      Supplier and product boxes; "Supply chain
                               concentration" (sole or single source).
    Government regulation      Violet regulatory boxes (regulators, orders,
                               licences); "Regulatory deadline approaching".
    Commodity risk             Amber macro boxes; "Commodity price exposure".
    Adverse competitive        COMPETES_WITH boxes for named competitors.
      dynamics
    Cyclicality, secular       Red risk and amber macro boxes when the
    decline, fad/fashion       filings' risk factors name them; "Risk factor
                               language change" flags new ones.
  Honest note: the first four have dedicated checks; the last three come from
  the filing's own risk factors, without a dedicated check.

STEP 3. FINANCIAL STATEMENT ANALYSIS                [FULL]  Financial and
                                                             Capital tabs
  (a) Profitability: Financial tab ("Step 3a"): revenue, EBITDA, margin, with
      eight quarters of charts.
  (b) Cash flow and liquidity: Financial tab: free cash flow, cash, current
      ratio against 1.0x, liquidity (cash plus revolver); runway on the
      watchlist.
  (c) Capital structure: Capital tab ("Step 3c"): debt, net debt, leverage,
      coverage, maturity wall, secured share.
  Gatto's red flags are built in as Devil's Advocate checks with the same
  names: Channel Stuffing, Absorption Costing, Understated Bad Debt, plus
  receivables versus revenue, DSO, DIO, SG&A growth and restricted cash.

STEP 4. FORECASTING                                  [FULL]  Changes tab,
                                                     "Forecast" section
  The question: management case, base case, downside case. The downside case
  is where you make or lose money.
  Does the AI predict anything? No. The AI never produces a future number.
  The forecast is computed by our code (lib/forecast.ts) from the company's
  own last eight quarters:
    Strong case    its best quarter of free cash flow repeats
    Base case      its average quarter over the last four repeats
    Downside case  its worst quarter repeats
  Cash is carried forward quarter by quarter for two years, debt principal is
  subtracted when the debt note says it is due, and no new borrowing or share
  sales are assumed. The result: cash after two years and the quarter the
  cash runs out, with every assumption printed beside it. For WULF: the base
  case runs out by June 2027, the downside by March 2027.
  The same table is an exhibit in the Devil's Advocate, so its downside
  argument cites these numbers, and "What to watch" names the filings that
  will confirm or break each case.
  Honest note: the "strong case" stands in for Gatto's management case, which
  would use management's own projections; most 10-Qs do not give numeric
  guidance, so we use the company's best recent quarter instead and say so.

STEP 5. CORPORATE VALUATION                         [PARTIAL]  Peers tab,
                                                               market data
  The question: comparable companies, precedent transactions, DCF,
  liquidation analysis, used together for a range.
  Where it is in the app
    - Comparable company analysis: Peers tab ("Step 5") puts leverage,
      coverage, liquidity and margins side by side.
    - Market value: market capitalization and the share price, which is the
      equity cushion beneath the lenders.
  Not yet: precedent transactions, DCF and liquidation analysis.

STEP 6. STRUCTURING AND DOCUMENTATION               [PARTIAL]  Capital tab,
                                                    Devil's Advocate checks
  The question: covenants, place in the capital structure, collateral,
  seniority.
  Where it is in the app
    - Seniority: each instrument's ranking (senior secured, senior unsecured,
      subordinated) and the secured share of total debt.
    - Place in the structure: "Subordination surprise" check (structurally or
      effectively subordinated debt).
    - Covenants: "Covenant headroom narrowing" when a filing states a maximum
      leverage ratio.
    - Credit support: "Guarantee scope narrower" (for WULF, Google's backstop
      starts only once each lease begins).
    - Private-company mode reads credit agreements directly.
  Not yet: a full covenant table and collateral description from the credit
  agreement itself.

STEP 7. INVESTMENT RECOMMENDATION                   [FULL]  Memo tab, "Step 7"
  The question: write the credit committee memo.
  Where it is in the app: the memo has exactly Gatto's five sections:
  Executive Summary, Thesis, Key Risks, Mitigants, Relative Value. Every name
  in it links to its evidence. Beyond the book: the Devil's Advocate case
  sits beside it, the meeting brief turns the gaps into questions for
  management, and "Copy memo" exports both.

WHAT TO SAY IF A JUDGE ASKS
  "We follow Gatto's seven steps, one tab per step. Qualitative analysis,

  Coverage at a glance
    1 Sources and uses          PARTIAL   Capital tab, 8-K 2.03, Changes
    2 Qualitative analysis      FULL      Graph tab, 8 risk categories
    3 Financial statements      FULL      Financial + Capital, red-flag checks
    4 Forecasting               FULL      Three cash cases, computed not guessed
    5 Valuation                 PARTIAL   Peers, market value
    6 Structuring               PARTIAL   Seniority, covenants, guarantees
    7 Recommendation            FULL      Memo in Gatto's five sections

PART 1. CREDIT ANALYSIS VOCABULARY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You will see these words in the code and in the UI.

  10-K           Annual report filed with the SEC. Longest, most complete.
  10-Q           Quarterly report. Shorter; often says "no material changes".
  8-K            Report of a specific event (new loan, CEO leaves, etc.).
                 Has "item codes": 5.02 = officer/director change,
                 4.01 = auditor change, 2.03 = new debt obligation.
  Form 4         Filed when an insider (officer, director) buys or sells stock.
  EDGAR          The SEC's public filing database. Free. Our main source.
  XBRL           Machine-readable tags inside filings. Every number in the
                 financial statements is tagged with a concept name such as
                 "Revenues" or "AccountsReceivableNetCurrent".
  Inline XBRL    The same tags embedded in the filing's HTML.
  Debt footnote  The note in a filing that lists the company's loans and bonds
  (debt note)    with amounts, interest rates (coupons) and due dates.
  Maturity       When a loan must be repaid. A "maturity wall" is a chart of
                 how much debt comes due each year.
  Coupon         The interest rate on a bond.
  Secured        Debt backed by collateral (paid first if things go wrong).
  Revolver       A credit line the company can draw on, like a credit card.
  EBITDA         Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization.
                 Rough proxy for cash the business generates.
  Leverage       Total debt / EBITDA. Higher = riskier.
  Coverage       EBITDA / interest expense. Lower = riskier.
  Liquidity      Cash plus undrawn revolver: what the company can pay with now.
  TTM / LTM      Trailing (last) twelve months.
  DSO            Days sales outstanding: how long customers take to pay.
  Counterparty   Any other party the company deals with.
  Gatto          Michael Gatto, author of a credit analysis book whose
                 seven-step process the tab labels follow.

PART 2.5  WHY THIS STACK (each choice, tied to the goal of the project)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The constraints that decided the stack:
  - A hackathon: a small team, a few days, one deployable app.
  - Trust: every number must be traceable to a primary source.
  - Zero paid data: judges must be able to run it, and a fund must be able to
    try it without a data contract.
  - Long, slow AI calls (30-60 seconds) that must not time out.
  - Rich, custom visuals (a map of boxes, charts) that follow strict design
    rules.

  Choice                      Why it fits the goal
  --------------------------  ----------------------------------------------
  Next.js 14 (App Router)     One codebase for the screens and the server.
                              The API routes (app/api/*) run the pipeline
                              next to the UI, so there is no second service
                              to build, deploy or keep in sync. Server code
                              keeps the API keys off the browser.

  TypeScript, strict          The Report object is large and every tab reads
                              it. Strict types catch a renamed field before
                              it breaks a screen; with no "any", the model's
                              JSON is checked before it is trusted.

  Tailwind CSS                Fast to build a consistent, dense UI. The
                              colors are CSS variables (app/globals.css), so
                              light and dark themes are one switch.

  React Flow (@xyflow/react)  The map needs zoom, pan, custom box components
                              and custom lines. React Flow gives the canvas;
                              we wrote our own layout (lib/layout.ts) because
                              physics layouts move every time and are hard to
                              read, and an analyst needs the same picture
                              every time.

  Custom SVG charts           The Financial charts, hover cards and bars are
                              drawn by our own components
                              (components/charts/trend-chart.tsx,
                              components/map/quarter-bars.tsx). That gives
                              full control: boxes not circles, our colors,
                              negative quarters in red, a hover readout with
                              q/q and y/y, and no heavy dependency. (recharts
                              and @dagrejs/dagre are still in package.json but
                              unused; they can be removed.)

  Google Gemini (REST, fetch) Gemini reads long filing sections in one call
                              (long context), returns JSON that matches a
                              schema we give it (structured output), and is
                              fast and inexpensive per call. We call it with
                              plain fetch in lib/gemini.ts, not an SDK or an
                              agent framework, so every request is visible and
                              easy to debug. A lighter fallback model takes
                              over when the main one is overloaded.

  No LangChain, no agents,    The model only reads and classifies; code does
  no vector database          the fetching, checking, math and citations. A
                              retrieval pipeline would add a layer that can
                              lose the exact source sentence, and proof of
                              source is the whole point. We send the relevant
                              filing sections directly instead.

  SEC EDGAR + XBRL            The primary source, free, legally required to be
                              accurate, and every number is tagged with its
                              filing. Paid data feeds summarize the same
                              filings; going to the source is what lets every
                              claim link to a sentence.

  Form 4 and 8-K item codes   Free, structured signals of behavior: insiders
                              selling, executives leaving, auditors changing.
                              They feed the Devil's Advocate.

  Google News RSS             Free, current headlines, so events between
                              filings (a lawsuit, a permit freeze) reach the
                              map. Headlines are labeled as news, never mixed
                              up with filings.

  yahoo-finance2              Free daily prices for the stock and the S&P 500.
                              The stock is the first place trouble shows,
                              before the next filing.

  Tiger Data (managed         Reports are saved as JSON (fast to reload, so a
  Postgres)                   repeat view costs nothing), and every box and
                              line is also written to entity and relationship
                              tables, so the graph can be queried across
                              companies ("every CUSTOMER_OF edge to Google")
                              without running a separate graph database.
                              Managed, so no database to operate.

  Vercel                      Deploys on every push, gives a public URL for
                              judges, and allows long functions (we set
                              maxDuration up to 300 seconds) for the Devil's
                              Advocate and the timeline, which call Gemini
                              for 30-60 seconds.

  Streaming progress          /api/analyze sends newline-delimited JSON events
                              as each step finishes, so a 15-40 second
                              analysis shows progress instead of a blank wait.

  unpdf (in the browser)      Private-company documents are read in the
                              user's browser; only their text is sent to
                              write the case, and nothing is stored. A lender's
                              documents are confidential, so the design keeps
                              the files on the user's machine.

  lucide-react                Plain line icons that match the sober look.
  framer-motion               Only for the side panel and toasts sliding in.

  Fonts: Geist, Geist Mono,   Geist for reading, Geist Mono for figures (they
  Space Grotesk               line up in columns), Space Grotesk for headings
                              and big numbers. Three fonts, no more.

What we deliberately did not choose, and why
  Python backend   A second service and deployment; the work is mostly I/O
                   (fetching, calling the model), which Node handles well.
  Neo4j            A graph database is overkill for one company's map; node
                   and edge tables in Postgres answer the queries we need.
  Paid data APIs   Cost, contracts and keys judges cannot use; and they are
                   one step removed from the source we want to cite.
  An AI that writes numbers  The model never supplies a figure. Numbers come
                   from XBRL, the debt note or the filing text, and generated
                   sentences with unknown numbers are deleted.

PART 4. THE JOURNEY OF ONE TICKER  (read this part twice)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Follow "WULF" from the keyboard to the screen.

Step 1. The user types WULF in the top bar.
  Read: components/layout/top-bar.tsx  (the form calls onAnalyze)
        app/page.tsx, function analyze()

  page.tsx sends POST /api/analyze { ticker: "WULF" }. The response is a
  stream of lines, each a small JSON event: {"type":"step",...} for progress,
  then {"type":"result","report":{...}} at the end. readEvents() in page.tsx
  reads that stream and shows progress in components/layout/pipeline-progress.tsx.

Step 2. The server route.
  Read: app/api/analyze/route.ts
  It checks the database for a recent report for this ticker (a cache). If none,
  it calls the pipeline in lib/analyze.ts and streams progress.

Step 3. The pipeline (lib/analyze.ts). In order:
  a. Look up the company's CIK (SEC id) from the ticker.     lib/edgar.ts
  b. Pick the latest 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K and download them.     lib/edgar.ts
     HTML is turned into plain text; sections such as Risk Factors and MD&A
     are cut out (extractSections) so we send Gemini the useful parts.
  c. Headlines from Google News, prices from Yahoo.            lib/news.ts, lib/market.ts
  d. XBRL numbers: revenue, cash, operating income...          lib/xbrl.ts
  e. Gemini call 1: "find the entities, with a quote for each" lib/extraction.ts
     Gemini call 2: "read the debt footnote"                   lib/capital.ts
  f. Verification: every quote must actually appear in the     lib/quotes.ts,
     document; every number in a label must be in its quote.     lib/citations.ts
     Rejected items are counted ("17/20 verified" in the status bar).

  - trailingTwelveMonths() builds TTM figures.

  - Reads debt tags (coupon, face amount, maturity) straight from the filing
    HTML, to fill gaps the footnote text leaves.

News (lib/news.ts) and market data (lib/market.ts)
  - Ten recent headlines; 90-day price change against the S&P 500.

PART 6. GEMINI, AND HOW WE STOP IT FROM MAKING THINGS UP
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Read: lib/gemini.ts, then lib/extraction.ts, then lib/quotes.ts.

  - generateJson(system, prompt, schema) sends a prompt and a JSON schema, and
    Gemini must answer in that shape. If the main model is busy (HTTP 429/503)
    it retries, then falls back to a lighter model.
  - Gemini only READS and CLASSIFIES. It never supplies a number or a quote
    we keep without checking.

The checks, which you will see again and again:
  1. Quote check: the quoted sentence must be found in the source document
     (allowing for whitespace and punctuation differences). Otherwise the
     entity is rejected.
  2. Figure check: extractFigures() pulls every number out of a label; each one
     must appear in the quote.
  3. Generated text check: dropUnsupported() in lib/memo.ts deletes any memo
     sentence containing a number that is not in the facts Gemini was given.
  4. Ranking words: "largest", "sole", "primary" are dropped unless a quote
     uses them.
  5. Severity and confidence are categories (high/medium/low), never numeric
     scores.

PART 10. THE DEVIL'S ADVOCATE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The project's signature feature: the app argues against its own memo.

Read in this order:
  types/devils-advocate.ts
  lib/devils-advocate-patterns.ts   the 36 checks
  lib/devils-evidence.ts            the evidence tables, timeline, coverage
  lib/devils-advocate.ts            the Gemini call
  app/api/devils-advocate/route.ts
  lib/counter-view.ts               helpers for the UI
  components/memo/counter-argument-column.tsx   the summary beside the memo
  components/memo/devils-case.tsx               the full case below the memo

How it works:
  1. loadPatternContext() gathers data: the latest filing and the one before,
     the latest 10-K, 8-K item codes, Form 4 insider trades, XBRL facts.
  2. runPatterns() runs 36 checks. Each returns "fired", "clear" or
     "not_evaluated" (when the data does not exist). Examples:
       - Receivables growing faster than revenue (XBRL)
       - DSO increase (XBRL)
       - Guarantee scope narrower ("only", "solely" next to "guarantee")
       - Insider selling cluster (3+ insiders selling within ~30 days, Form 4)
       - Auditor change (8-K item 4.01)
       - New risk language versus the previous filing
  3. buildEvidence() (lib/devils-evidence.ts) builds, without the model:
       - the period: which quarter (e.g. Q2 2026), its end date, which filing,
         when it was filed and how many days ago
       - evidence tables ("exhibits") by theme, 8 quarters each, with the
         change against the prior quarter and the year-earlier quarter
         already computed: revenue, EBITDA, margin, interest, receivables,
         cash, current ratio, operating cash flow, capital spending, free
         cash flow, the debt and when it is due, the map's entities, what
         changed, insider trades, 8-Ks (item codes in words), peers, market
       - a dated timeline of filings, 8-Ks, insider trades and debt due dates
       - "what was read": every source, and which ones were not available
  4. Gemini gets all of that plus the memo, the patterns and the filing text,
     and writes a long case in fixed themes, in this order:
       earnings, liquidity, debt, counterparties, changes, behavior, peers,
       market
     Each section has: a headline, what the memo says, the counter-argument
     (150-250 words, naming quarters and dates), "in plain English", "why it
     matters to a lender", a strength (strong, moderate, weak) and sources.
     Then: a primer ("read this first"), the bottom line, the other side (the
     best answer to the case), and what to watch with when it will be known.
  5. Checks: every sentence with a number not in the evidence is removed; a
     theme with no evidence table gets no section; source labels and links
     are built by our code, never written by the model. A glossary of the
     terms the case actually uses is added from a fixed list.
  6. It also classifies the memo's own stance (Stable, Caution, At risk,
     Distressed) for the badge in the top bar.

Where it shows: the top-bar badge, the right column of the Memo tab (a
summary), the full case below the memo, the "Counter view" toggle on the
Graph tab, and a red card at the top of the Financial, Capital, Changes and
Peers tabs showing the matching section. Add peers on the Peers tab and press
"Argue again" to bring them into the case.

Private companies (/private, lib/devils-private.ts):
  Private companies file nothing with the SEC, so the user uploads their
  documents (credit agreement, lender report, offering memorandum) and writes
  the thesis. PDFs are read in the browser with unpdf; only the text is sent,
  and nothing is saved. The text checks run on the documents; checks that need
  public data (Form 4s, 8-Ks, XBRL, stock price) are marked not evaluated.
  Gemini first lists the key figures and dated events; a figure is kept only
  if its value is found in the document it names. Then the same structured
  case is written, citing documents as doc1, doc2...
  Check 36, "Receivables Sold or Supplier Finance", looks for factoring and
  supplier finance: debt-like financing that does not show as debt.

Test it from the command line:
  npx tsx --env-file=.env.local scripts/run-patterns.ts <report id>
  npx tsx --env-file=.env.local scripts/run-private.ts "<company>" thesis.txt doc1.txt [doc2.txt]
  npx tsx --env-file=.env.local scripts/run-case.ts <report id> [peer ids]

PART 10.5  WHY THE DEVIL'S ADVOCATE EXISTS, AND WHY EVERY PART OF IT IS THERE
           (read this before any demo; judges ask about it most)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. WHY BUILD A DEVIL'S ADVOCATE AT ALL

  The problem it solves
    A credit memo is an argument for a conclusion. Whoever writes it, human or
    AI, tends to collect the evidence that supports it and pass over the
    evidence that does not. That is confirmation bias, and in credit it is
    expensive: a lender's upside is capped at getting paid back with interest,
    while the downside can be most of the loan. A credit fund makes its money
    by avoiding the few bad loans, so the evidence against a deal is worth more
    than the evidence for it.

    Credit losses usually come from details, not headlines: a guarantee that
    only starts after construction, debt-like financing that is not counted as
    debt, receivables piling up faster than sales, insiders selling while the
    memo says all is well. These sit in footnotes, 8-Ks and Form 4s that
    nobody has time to read for 60 companies.

  Why an AI tool especially needs one
    Our own pipeline writes the memo. An AI that writes a confident memo and
    stops there makes confirmation bias faster, not smaller. So the app argues
    against its own conclusion, with the same sources, before the analyst
    relies on it.

  Why it is the centerpiece, not a side feature
    Summarizing filings is something many tools do. Investment committees
    already use a "red team" or a devil's advocate in the room: one person is
    asked to make the case against the deal. Signal Watch automates that role,
    which is the part of the process that is skipped first when people are
    busy. It is what makes the product a decision tool rather than a reader.

  What it is not
    It is not a rating, a score, a price prediction or a "sell" signal. It is
    the strongest honest case against the memo, with its evidence, so the
    analyst can answer it or change their mind.

2. WHY 36 CHECKS RUN IN CODE BEFORE THE AI WRITES ANYTHING

  The checks (lib/devils-advocate-patterns.ts) are computed by our code from
  XBRL figures, the filing text, 8-K item codes and Form 4 trades. Gemini
  only writes the argument afterwards. Reasons:
    - Repeatable: the same data always gives the same result.
    - Auditable: each check shows its numbers or the sentence it matched, with
      a link.
    - Not invented: a check fires only when the numbers or words are actually
      there.
    - Expertise in code: each check encodes a known red flag that credit and
      forensic-accounting analysts look for.

  Three results, and why the third matters
    Fired          The warning condition holds in the data.
    Clear          The data was read and the condition does not hold.
    Not evaluated  The data the check needs is not available.
  Most tools would quietly treat "no data" as "no problem". We never do: a
  missing number is shown as not evaluated, so the analyst knows exactly what
  was not checked.

3. EVERY CHECK, WHAT IT CATCHES, AND WHY A LENDER CARES

  Financial statements (from XBRL, the company's own tagged numbers)
    Receivables growing faster than revenue
        Sales booked but not collected. Can mean aggressive revenue
        recognition or customers who cannot pay; often precedes write-downs.
    Inventory growing faster than cost of goods sold
        Goods are not selling; inventory may need writing down, tying up cash.
    SG&A growing faster than revenue
        Overhead outrunning the business; margins will shrink.
    DSO increase (days sales outstanding)
        Customers are taking longer to pay: cash arrives later than profit.
    DIO increase (days inventory outstanding)
        Inventory sits longer: cash is stuck on shelves.
    Absorption costing
        Producing more than is sold parks costs in inventory and flatters
        margins for a while.
    Channel stuffing
        A jump in receivables far beyond the quarter's revenue growth: sales
        may have been pushed into the channel early.
    Understated bad debt
        Receivables growing while the allowance for bad debts does not: future
        losses not yet recognized.
    Restricted cash growth
        Cash the company reports but cannot freely use; liquidity is smaller
        than it looks.
    Off-balance-sheet liabilities
        Large lease obligations outside the debt total: reported leverage
        understates what the company owes.

  Customers and revenue
    Concentration with undisclosed terms
        A customer of 20% of revenue or more, with no stated contract term or
        termination right: the biggest revenue line could leave.
    Anchor tenant loss risk
        An anchor tenant named without its lease expiry.
    Customer churn mentioned but not quantified
        Management says churn but gives no number: a gap to question.
    Revenue per customer declining         (not evaluated: no standard tag)
    Top customer rating risk               (not evaluated: no ratings feed)
        Both are shown as not evaluated so the gap is visible.

  Debt and capital structure
    Covenant headroom narrowing
        Leverage close to the loan agreement's maximum: a breach lets lenders
        demand repayment or new terms.
    Near-term maturity concentration
        More than 40% of principal due within two years: refinancing risk.
    Floating rate exposure
        Most debt pays a floating rate; each 1% rate rise costs real cash.
    Guarantee scope narrower
        Wording such as "only", "solely" or "does not extend" next to a
        guarantee or backstop. On WULF it found that Google's backstop of
        Fluidstack's leases "is only effective following the commencement of
        the relevant lease": the construction period is not covered.
    Subordination surprise
        Debt that is structurally or effectively subordinated: those lenders
        are paid after others, whatever the bond's name says.
    Pension underfunded
        Pension obligations above plan assets: a debt-like claim.
    Receivables sold or supplier finance
        Factoring, receivables sales or supplier-finance programs. They raise
        cash or stretch payments without being counted as debt, so leverage
        can be understated. This kind of hidden financing is behind several
        well-known private-credit collapses, which is why it was added.

  Management and operations (filing language, 8-Ks, Form 4s)
    Management tone shift
        Growth words falling and caution words rising per 10,000 words against
        the previous filing of the same form.
    Risk factor language change
        Risk sentences in this filing that were not in the previous one: what
        management has newly started to worry about.
    Insider selling cluster
        Three or more insiders selling on the open market within about two
        weeks of each other (Form 4s from the last 60 days).
    Guidance raised but insiders selling
        The company says things are better while insiders sell.
    Key employee departures
        Two or more 8-Ks (item 5.02) in a year reporting officers leaving.
    Related-party transactions
        Deals with insiders or affiliates, with amounts: check the terms.
    Auditor change
        An 8-K item 4.01. Changing auditors often comes before restatements.
    Change in accounting estimate
        For example longer useful lives for equipment, which lowers expenses
        and lifts reported earnings without any change in the business.
    Litigation reserve missing
        Litigation disclosed with no amount set aside.

  Industry and macro
    Supply chain concentration
        A sole or single-source supplier: one disruption stops the business.
    Regulatory deadline approaching
        A compliance date within twelve months.
    Commodity price exposure
        A named input cost that is 20% or more of cost of revenue.
    Currency exposure
        A large share of revenue in foreign currencies.
    Customer concentration by sector      (not evaluated: needs customers'
        industries, which the filings do not give)

4. WHY THE EVIDENCE TABLES ARE BUILT BY CODE (lib/devils-evidence.ts)

  Before Gemini writes, our code assembles "exhibits": quarter-by-quarter
  tables of revenue, EBITDA, margin, interest, receivables, cash, current
  ratio, operating cash flow, capital spending and free cash flow (8
  quarters, with the change from the prior quarter and from a year earlier
  already worked out), the debt and when it is due, the map's entities, what
  changed, insider trades, 8-Ks with item codes in words, peers and market
  data.
  Why:
    - The model is not allowed to calculate. Every percentage it may cite is
      already in a table, so the number check can verify it.
    - The same tables are shown on screen under each section ("The
      evidence"), so the reader sees the data behind each claim.
    - It is how "use all the data we gathered" is enforced: every tab's data
      reaches the argument.

5. WHY THE CASE IS SPLIT INTO THESE EIGHT THEMES, IN THIS ORDER

  1 Revenue and earnings quality   Can the business generate money?
  2 Cash and liquidity             Can it pay its bills now?
  3 Debt, maturities, refinancing  What does it owe, and when?
  4 Customers, suppliers, guarantors  Who could break the plan?
  5 What changed this quarter      Is it getting better or worse?
  6 Management and insider behavior  Do insiders act like they believe it?
  7 Against the peers              Is it weak compared with similar companies?
  8 What the stock market is saying  Is the market already pricing trouble?

  The order follows how a lender judges repayment: first the ability to
  generate cash, then the cushion, then the obligations, then the outside
  dependencies, then direction, behavior and comparison. A theme with no
  evidence table gets no section, so the case never pads itself with empty
  claims.

6. WHY EACH SECTION HAS THESE PARTS

  Headline            The point as one sentence, so a reader skimming the
                      section list gets the whole case.
  What the memo says  The memo's own claim that this section answers (or "The
                      memo does not address this"). It forces the case to
                      engage with the memo, not argue with a straw man, and it
                      shows the reader what the memo left out.
  The counter-argument  150-250 words built from the evidence, naming quarters
                      with their end dates ("Q2 2026, the quarter ended
                      2026-06-30") and comparing with the prior quarter and the
                      year-earlier quarter. Time is in every argument because
                      credit is about direction: a loss that is shrinking and
                      one that is growing are different credits.
  In plain English    2-3 sentences for someone with no finance background,
                      with every term defined. Investment committee members,
                      judges and new teammates are not all credit specialists;
                      a warning that cannot be understood is ignored.
  Why it matters to a lender  1-2 sentences linking the point to repayment.
                      Every finding is translated into "how could we lose
                      money", which is the only question that matters here.
  Strength            Strong, moderate or weak: a category, never a number, so
                      the case ranks its own points without fake precision.
                      The badge counts strong and moderate points as concerns.
  Sources             Built by our code from the entity, check or document
                      cited, never written by the model.
  The evidence        The exhibit tables for that theme.

7. WHY THE PARTS AROUND THE SECTIONS ARE THERE

  The period line     "Based on Q2 2026, the quarter ended 2026-06-30 · 10-Q
                      filed 2026-08-05, 67 days before this case was written."
                      Stale information is a classic way to be wrong; the age
                      of the data is stated up front.
  Read this first     A short primer: what the company does, which filing, what
                      the memo concludes, what the case argues instead. Someone
                      who opens the page cold knows what they are reading.
  Bottom line         The whole case in one paragraph, strongest points first,
                      for the reader with one minute.
  Timeline            Filings, important 8-Ks, insider trades and debt due
                      dates, oldest first, with future dates marked upcoming.
                      It shows when things happened in relation to each other
                      (for example insiders selling after a filing).
  The other side      The best answer to the case, from facts such as checks
                      that came back clear. A tool that only attacks is easy
                      to dismiss; showing the rebuttal is what makes it fair
                      and credible.
  What to watch, and when  Each item says what to check, when it will be known
                      (the next 10-Q, a maturity date) and what result would
                      confirm or weaken the case. It turns the argument into
                      a monitoring plan.
  Terms used          Definitions of only the terms the case uses, from a fixed
                      list written by us, not the model.
  What was read, and what was not  Every source read and every source that
                      was not (for example "Peers: not compared"). A case is
                      only as good as its inputs; we show the blind spots.
  Every check         All 36 checks with fired, clear or not evaluated.

8. WHY IT CAN BE TRUSTED (the safeguards)

  - Numbers: any sentence containing a figure that is not in the evidence is
    deleted, and the count of deleted sentences is shown.
  - No hedging words: "likely", "probably" and "may" are rejected; the case
    must state what the filings show or say a thing is not disclosed.
  - Retries: if too many sentences are dropped or hedges appear, Gemini is
    asked again with a reminder, and the better attempt is kept.
  - Citations: the model names an entity id, a check or a source key; our code
    turns that into the label and link. The model cannot invent a source.
  - No ratings, scores or price predictions, by rule.
  - The memo's stance (Stable, Caution, At risk, Distressed) is a category
    describing the memo, shown beside the count of concerns against it.

9. WHY IT APPEARS IN SO MANY PLACES

  Top bar badge     "Memo: Stable · 7 concerns": the dual verdict follows the
                    analyst to every tab.
  Beside the memo   A summary column (period, primer, bottom line, the points
                    with their strength, what to watch) next to the memo it
                    argues with.
  Below the memo    The full case with the evidence tables.
  Counter view      On the map, the flagged boxes turn red: where the risk sits
                    among the company's relationships.
  Cards on tabs     Financial shows the earnings and liquidity points; Capital
                    the debt points; Changes the change and behavior points;
                    Peers the peer points. The challenge appears where the
                    analyst is looking, not on a separate page they may skip.
  Runs automatically  Every report gets its case when it loads; an analyst
                    should not have to remember to ask for the counter-view.
  Copy memo         Exports the memo and the full case together, so the
                    challenge travels with the memo into the committee.

10. WHY THE PRIVATE COMPANY MODE (/private)

  Many borrowers in private credit and leveraged loans are private: no 10-K,
  no XBRL, no Form 4s. Their information sits in credit agreements, lender
  reports and offering memoranda. Without this mode, the tool would be blind
  exactly where many credit losses happen.
  How it is built, and why:
    - Files are read in the browser and only text is sent; nothing is saved.
      Lender documents are confidential.
    - The text checks run on the documents unchanged (guarantee scope,
      subordination, related parties, receivables sold, litigation...).
    - Checks that need public data (insider trades, 8-Ks, XBRL, stock price)
      are marked not evaluated, never clear.
    - Gemini first lists the key figures and dated events; a figure is kept
      only if its value is found in the document it names. Then the same
      structured case is written, citing documents as doc1, doc2.

11. WHAT IT COSTS, AND ITS LIMITS (say these before a judge does)

  - About 30-60 seconds per company; the result is saved on the report, so
    later views are instant.
  - It is only as good as its inputs: if peers or the changes comparison were
    not run, those themes are thin, and "What was read" says so.
  - Strength and the memo stance are model judgments, shown as categories.
  - Several checks need data no free source gives (customer counts, customer
    ratings, customer industries); they are shown as not evaluated.
  - A fired check is a question to investigate, not proof of wrongdoing.

PART 13. THE RULES
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Honesty rules (most important):
  - Never invent data. No made-up numbers, ratings, spreads or examples.
  - Every box has a verified source quote.
  - Missing data is labeled ("Not disclosed", "No bond price feed",
    "not evaluated") instead of hidden or guessed.
  - Severity and confidence are categories, not numeric scores.
  - No price predictions.

Design rules:
  - Fonts: Geist, Geist Mono, Space Grotesk only.
  - Colors: zinc grays plus the six entity colors (blue company, red risk,
    amber macro, emerald financial, violet regulatory, gray other).
  - Boxes, not circles. No drop shadows, no emoji, no gradient text.
  - Animations play once and settle (the faint canvas wash is the only loop,
    and it is off for users who prefer reduced motion).

Working rules: commit after each step, run the type check, test with WULF.

PART 16. GLOSSARY OF PROJECT TERMS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Report          Everything built for one ticker at one time.
  Entity / node   One box on the map.
  Central node    The company itself, in the middle.
  Spoke           A line from the company to an entity.
  Magnitude       A disclosed figure attached to an entity, with its quote.
  Severity        How much an entity matters to credit (high/medium/low/none),
                  rated by Gemini.
  Confidence      How sure we are the relationship is real (high/medium/low),
                  from the source and wording.
  Validation      Counts of entities Gemini proposed, kept and rejected.
  Baseline        The report rebuilt from last quarter's filings, for Changes.
  Timeline        Which past filings name each entity.
  Pattern         One of the 36 Devil's Advocate checks.
  Counter view    The Graph tab mode that highlights flagged boxes in red.
  Stance          The memo's own position: Stable, Caution, At risk, Distressed.
  Aggregate debt  Debt reported only by category (banks), not per bond.
  n/m             Not meaningful (e.g. leverage when EBITDA is negative).


------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 17. 60 QUESTIONS JUDGES WILL ASK, WITH ANSWERS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Answer in your own words; these are the facts to stand on. If you do not know
something, say so. Judges trust "we did not build that yet" more than a guess.

A. THE PRODUCT AND THE PROBLEM

1. What problem does this solve?
   A credit analyst covers 50-70 companies and spends hours per company
   reading filings to write a memo. Signal Watch does the reading up front:
   ticker in, a sourced map, debt picture, memo and counter-argument out, with
   every claim linked to the filing sentence.

2. Who is the user?
   A credit analyst or portfolio manager at a credit fund, who reads 10-Ks,
   10-Qs and 8-Ks and writes memos for an investment committee.

3. How is this different from asking ChatGPT about a company?
   A chatbot answers from memory and can invent numbers. Signal Watch reads
   the actual current filings, keeps only claims whose quote is found in the
   filing, links every claim to its source, and labels what is not disclosed.

4. How is it different from Ripple (or other graph tools)?
   Ripple monitors markets and events. Signal Watch produces the analyst's
   work product, the credit memo, plus a Devil's Advocate that argues against
   it. Our boxes carry the credit view (severity, debt, maturities).

5. Why a graph and not a table?
   Credit risk is about dependencies. A map shows concentration (one customer,
   one guarantor, one power supplier) and chains of exposure at a glance.
   Every box still opens its exact quote, so nothing is lost versus a table.

6. What is the Devil's Advocate and why does it matter?
   It argues against the memo. 36 checks run on real data (XBRL figures,
   filing text, 8-K item codes, Form 4 insider trades), then Gemini writes a
   cited counter-argument. It fights confirmation bias, which is how credit
   funds lose money.

7. Why follow Gatto's seven steps?
   It is a standard credit analysis framework, so an analyst recognizes the
   flow: qualitative analysis, profitability, capital structure, forecasting,
   valuation, recommendation. Each tab is one step.

8. What is the time dimension for?
   Credit is about direction. The timeline shows which of the last twelve
   filings name each entity, so you see whether a risk is new or persistent;
   every tab shows an "as of" date so old numbers are never mistaken for new.

B. DATA

9. Where does the data come from?
   SEC EDGAR (filings and Form 4s), SEC XBRL company facts (tagged financial
   numbers), Google News RSS (headlines), Yahoo Finance (prices). All public.

10. Why SEC filings rather than a paid data provider?
    They are the primary source, free, legally required to be accurate, and
    every number can be traced. Paid feeds summarize the same filings.

11. What is XBRL and why use it?
    Machine-readable tags on every number in the financial statements. We use
    it for revenue, EBITDA inputs, cash, debt and quarterly history, so those
    numbers come straight from the company's own filing, not from AI.

12. How fresh is the data?
    Each analysis reads the latest filings at that moment. Saved reports are
    reused for 12 hours; "Read the filings again" forces a fresh read. The
    dashboard flags companies that filed after their last report.

13. What about bond prices and spreads?
    We do not have a bond price feed, and the app says so ("No bond price
    feed") instead of showing made-up spreads. Adding TRACE or a vendor feed
    is a clear next step.

14. Credit ratings?
    Shown only when a filing or headline states them, with the sentence.
    Otherwise "Rating not stated".

C. AI AND ACCURACY

15. Which AI model do you use and why?
    Google Gemini (Flash, with a lighter fallback when it is overloaded). It
    has a long context window for whole filing sections, structured JSON
    output, and is fast and inexpensive per call.

16. How do you stop hallucinations?
    Gemini only reads and classifies. Every entity must quote a sentence that
    we find in the source document, or it is rejected. Every number in a label
    must appear in its quote. Any generated sentence (memo, brief, answers,
    counter-argument) containing a number not in the facts is deleted.

17. What does "17/20 verified" in the status bar mean?
    Gemini proposed 20 entities; 17 passed the quote check; 3 were rejected.
    Clicking it lists what was rejected and why. We show our failures.

18. How do you measure accuracy?
    Every kept claim is verified against its quote by code, and rejections are
    logged. We tested by hand on WULF, CIFR, IREN, Ford and JPMorgan. A formal
    precision/recall benchmark needs a hand-labeled answer key; that is
    future work, and we will not quote a number we have not measured.

19. Can the severity rating be wrong?
    Yes. Severity is Gemini's judgment, shown as a category (high, medium,
    low), never a numeric score, and the box shows the quote so the analyst
    can disagree. Facts (amounts, dates, names) are verified; judgments are
    labeled as judgments.

20. What if Gemini is down or rate-limited?
    It retries, then falls back to a lighter model. If drafting fails, the
    memo is assembled from the map without AI text, and the app says so.

21. How does the Devil's Advocate avoid making things up?
    Its patterns are computed by code from XBRL, filing text, 8-K items and
    Form 4s; a pattern fires only when the numbers or wording are found, and
    says "not evaluated" when data is missing. Gemini's argument is filtered
    for unsupported numbers, and citations are resolved by our code to real
    entities, patterns or the debt note, never written by the model.

22. Is "Memo: Stable" a rating?
    No. It is Gemini's classification of the stance the memo takes, shown so
    the analyst sees the memo's view next to the count of sourced concerns.

D. ARCHITECTURE AND TECH

23. Walk us through the architecture.
    Next.js app. The browser calls POST /api/analyze, which streams progress.
    The server pipeline fetches filings, news, market data and XBRL, calls
    Gemini for entities and debt in parallel, verifies quotes, builds the
    graph, drafts the memo and saves a Report to Postgres. Every tab renders
    from that Report; extra pieces (timeline, changes, peers, brief, Devil's
    Advocate) are separate API routes.

24. Why Next.js?
    One codebase for the React UI and the server API routes, TypeScript end
    to end, and simple deployment on Vercel.

25. Why stream progress instead of one request?
    An analysis takes 15-40 seconds. Streaming newline-delimited JSON events
    lets the UI show each step (fetching filings, extracting, verifying,
    writing) instead of a blank wait.

26. Why React Flow for the map?
    It handles zoom, pan, custom node components and edges. We wrote our own
    layout (company in the middle, boxes on four sides by type) because
    force-directed layouts move every time and are hard to read.

27. What database and why?
    Tiger Data (managed Postgres). Reports are stored as JSON, and entities
    and relationships also go into node and edge tables, so we can query the
    graph across companies (for example every CUSTOMER_OF edge). Postgres
    gives us that without running a separate graph database.

28. How do you respect SEC rate limits?
    A shared throttle keeps us under 10 requests per second with a proper
    User-Agent, and responses are cached.

29. How long does it take and how does it scale?
    15-40 seconds for a fresh analysis, near-instant for a saved one. The
    timeline builds in seconds and is saved; the full Devil's Advocate
    case takes 30-60 seconds and is saved on the report. Analyses are
    independent, so they scale horizontally on serverless functions; the
    limits are SEC rate limits and model quotas.

30. What does it cost to run?
    A handful of Gemini calls per company (entities, debt, memo, plus the
    Devil's Advocate), with all data sources free. Saved reports are reused,
    so repeat views cost nothing.

31. How is the code kept safe and maintainable?
    Strict TypeScript with no "any", shared types in types/, logic in lib/
    separate from UI components, a type check and lint before every commit.
    API keys live in environment variables, never in the repo.

32. How do you handle different companies' filing formats?
    HTML is converted to text and sections are found by their Item headings.
    XBRL tag names vary between companies and years, so we try several
    concepts and pick the series that reaches the latest date. Banks that
    report debt only by category are detected and labeled.

33. How did you get debt for a company like JPMorgan?
    Its footnote reports debt by category, not by bond. We read every
    category row with its weighted average rate, mark them as aggregate,
    fill the maturity schedule from its XBRL tags, and say so on screen.

E. BUSINESS, LIMITS, NEXT STEPS

34. Would a fund actually use this?
    Yes: it fits the existing workflow (EDGAR, the memo, the IC meeting) and
    produces the deliverable rather than another dashboard. Every claim is
    auditable, which is the bar for acting on it.

35. Does it replace the analyst?
    No. It does the reading and the first draft; the analyst checks the
    sources, disagrees with ratings, and makes the call. The Devil's Advocate
    is there to make the analyst think harder, not less.

36. What are the biggest limitations today?
    No bond prices or spreads; severity is a model judgment; very long filings
    are excerpted; private companies get the Devil's Advocate from uploaded
    documents but not the map or the financial tabs; no formal accuracy
    benchmark yet.

37. What would you build next?
    A bond price and spread feed, a hand-labeled accuracy benchmark, alerts
    that re-run the analysis when a company files, and covenant extraction
    from credit agreements.

38. What was the hardest technical problem?
    Trustworthy numbers: XBRL tag names change over time, debt footnotes vary
    by company, and the model must never supply a figure. The fix was layered
    verification (quote check, figure check, reconciliation of total debt
    against the filing's own table or XBRL).

39. What would you do with more time?
    Formal accuracy testing on a labeled set of companies, covenant headroom
    from credit agreements, and team features (shared watchlists, comments).

40. What did you learn?
    For finance, trust beats features: a feature users cannot check gets
    ignored. Showing sources, saying "not disclosed", and showing our own
    rejection count made the output usable.

F. THE DEVIL'S ADVOCATE, IN DEPTH

41. Why did you build a Devil's Advocate at all?
    Because the memo is an argument for a conclusion, and confirmation bias is
    how lenders lose money. A lender's upside is capped and the downside is
    large, so the evidence against a deal matters more. Investment committees
    already assign someone to argue against a deal; we automated that role.

42. Isn't it just a prompt that says "be negative"?
    No. 36 checks run in code first, on XBRL figures, filing text, 8-K item
    codes and Form 4 trades. Our code also builds the evidence tables
    (quarter by quarter, with changes already computed). Gemini only writes
    the argument from that evidence, and every sentence with a number not in
    the evidence is deleted. Sources are attached by code.

43. Why should an analyst trust it?
    Every point shows its evidence table and links to the filing, XBRL concept
    or Form 4. Missing data is shown as "not evaluated", not as clear. And it
    shows the other side: the best answer to its own case.

44. What if it is wrong?
    Then the analyst can see why, because the evidence is on screen. A fired
    check is a question to investigate, not proof. The cost of a false alarm
    is a few minutes of reading; the cost of a missed one can be the loan.

45. Why explain everything in plain English?
    Committee members, judges and new analysts are not all credit specialists.
    A warning nobody understands gets ignored. Each section has a plain-English
    version and a "why it matters to a lender" line, and the terms used are
    defined at the end.

46. Why are quarters and dates in every argument?
    Credit is about direction. A loss that is shrinking and one that is growing
    are different credits, and old data is a common way to be wrong. Each case
    states which quarter and filing it uses and how old that filing is.

47. Why not give a risk score from 1 to 100?
    A number would suggest precision we do not have. Strength is strong,
    moderate or weak, and the memo's stance is a category. The numbers we do
    show are the company's own reported figures.

48. Why does it include a rebuttal against itself?
    A tool that only attacks is easy to dismiss. Showing the strongest answer
    to the case, from checks that came back clear and strengths in the tables,
    makes it fair and lets the analyst weigh both sides.

49. Why does it take a minute?
    It reads the latest and previous filings, Form 4s, 8-Ks and XBRL facts,
    runs 36 checks, builds the tables, then writes eight sections in one
    structured call. It runs automatically when a report loads and is saved,
    so later views are instant.

50. Doesn't it overwhelm the analyst with text?
    It is layered: the top bar badge (one line), the summary column beside the
    memo (bottom line and the list of points with their strength), then the
    full case for whoever needs it. Each tab shows only the points about that
    tab.

51. What did it find on a real company?
    On WULF (TeraWulf), from the 10-K, that Google's backstop of Fluidstack's
    leases is only effective once each lease commences, so the construction
    period is not covered; receivables up 1026.5% year over year; free cash
    flow of -$992.3 million in Q2 2026; and three insiders selling within weeks.
    The memo, written from the same filings, rated the credit Stable.

52. How do you know the 36 checks are the right ones?
    They are standard red flags from credit and forensic accounting work
    (receivables versus revenue, DSO, covenant headroom, maturity walls,
    subordination, auditor changes, insider selling). Each was tested on real
    companies; false positives we found (for example an accounting-policy
    sentence read as a change in estimate) were fixed by tightening the rule.

53. Does it work for private companies?
    Yes, at /private. The analyst uploads the credit agreement, lender report
    or offering memo and writes the thesis. Files are read in the browser and
    nothing is saved. Text checks run on the documents; checks that need
    public data say "not evaluated". Figures are kept only if found in the
    document named.

54. Why the "Receivables sold or supplier finance" check?
    Factoring and supplier finance raise cash or delay payments without being
    counted as debt, so leverage can look lower than it is. Hidden financing of
    this kind is behind several well-known private-credit collapses.

55. What would make it better?
    Bond prices and spreads (so the market section covers lenders, not just
    shareholders), lease terms set against debt maturities, a counterparty
    tier for each tenant (hyperscaler, neocloud, AI lab), and a labeled set of
    past defaults to measure how early the checks fire.

G. DESIGN AND THE NEWER FEATURES

56. Why hover cards on the map?
    The map shows relationships; the hover card brings the numbers to them.
    Hovering the company shows eight quarters of revenue, margin and cash with
    the credit ratios; hovering any other box shows its figure, share, debt
    terms, the quarters whose filings name it, and its quote.

57. Why are some lines thicker and some labels red?
    Line thickness follows the dollar amount, so the biggest exposures stand
    out. Labels carry the figure and, for debt, the year it is due; red means
    due within two years, which is refinancing risk.

58. Why custom charts instead of a chart library?
    Control and consistency: boxes not circles, our six colors, losses in red,
    and a readout with the change from the prior quarter and from a year
    earlier. They are small SVG components with no extra dependency.

59. What do the arrows on the map mean?
    Read from the end without the arrow, through the label, to the end with
    it: "Anthropic CUSTOMER OF TeraWulf" points at TeraWulf; "TeraWulf
    REGULATED BY the Kentucky PSC" points at the regulator. Without a
    direction, "CUSTOMER OF" could mean money in or money out. Part 0.7 lists
    every other symbol.

60. How does the watchlist help a hedge-fund analyst?
    An analyst covers 50-70 companies; each morning they need to know which
    changed and which to deal with first. The watchlist checks each company
    against EDGAR and its XBRL figures and sorts it into Act now, Review or
    Quiet by written rules (a dangerous 8-K, cash covering under four quarters
    of burn, debt due soon, the Devil's Advocate disagreeing with a Stable
    memo), each with its dated, linked reason. It tracks each case's "what to
    watch" items until the filing that settles them arrives.
